About QMSBR

A common architecture for learning quantitative research

The purpose, argument framework, authorship, citation, licensing, and accessibility commitments of QMSBR.

QMSBR—Quantitative Methods for Social and Behavioral Research—is a modular collection of chapters designed to support more than one quantitative-methods course. Reproducible computing resources may join later releases after separate review. The collection is maintained as one canonical library so a correction can improve every course route that uses it.

The framework

The Argument-Based Framework for Quantitative Research organizes the work needed to answer a quantitative question in empirical research. The quantitative question sits at the centre and is the organizing unit. An optional, preferably prespecified hypothesis is a candidate answer; the final answer is a bounded substantive claim whose strength and scope reflect the evidence, design, assumptions, uncertainty, context, and reservations.

The framework distinguishes two linked four-part arguments:

Statistical argument Governing question
STAT-E · Empirical evidence What data and data-production information were recorded for which units?
STAT-J · Statistical justification Why can those records and the planned or fitted procedure address the estimand or predictive target, under which conditions?
STAT-I · Statistical inference What estimand, estimate, uncertainty, checking, sensitivity, multiplicity, and analytic status connect the evidence to a claim?
STAT-C · Bounded statistical claim What qualified statement is justified, with which statistical reservations?

Within each argument, Evidence and Justification jointly support Inference, which supports a bounded Claim. Across the two arguments, each substantive component develops its statistical counterpart: SUB-E situates STAT-E, SUB-J augments and evaluates STAT-J, SUB-I interprets STAT-I, and STAT-C bounds and calibrates SUB-C.

Substantive argument Governing question
SUB-E · Contextualized empirical evidence What do the cases, variables, and design information in STAT-E represent in their substantive population, setting, and period?
SUB-J · Substantive justification Why does this contextualized evidence bear on the substantive question, claim type, and scope?
SUB-I · Substantive inference and interpretation What does the result mean in substantive units and context, how important is it, and what alternatives remain?
SUB-C · Bounded substantive claim (bounded answer) What final bounded answer is justified, including reservations and limits on causal or population generalization?

The same framework has three coordinated perspectives: Argument asks what must logically justify the answer; Workflow asks how the argument is built and revised across eight iterative proposal and implementation stages; and Evaluation uses seven named lenses—Question alignment, Evidence integrity, Justification adequacy, Inferential adequacy, Transition fidelity, Claim calibration, and Cross-question coherence. Multiple questions may share evidence and support, but each requires a distinct traceable argument and answer outcome.

The framework is Toulmin-informed: it retains the need for evidence, a justified inferential bridge, qualified claims, and explicit reservations without treating the eight components as one-to-one replacements for Toulmin’s six roles. LeMire (2010) provides a related statistical precedent focused on null-hypothesis testing. Chapter 1’s Section 6, How the Regression-Based Approach Develops and Instantiates the Framework, explains how regression develops the available targets, comparisons, models, checks, and interpretive obligations, and how a particular regression analysis instantiates all eight components. Its Chapters 2–14 map is a subordinate guide to that development.

Scope

Part One focuses mainly on observable variables, foundational statistical reasoning, R, and regression. Factor analysis and latent-variable modelling are reserved for Part Two; multilevel and longitudinal models are reserved for Part Three. Study design is addressed wherever it controls interpretation, but regression alone never creates a causal claim.

Accessibility

HTML is the preferred reading format: it provides responsive layout, headings, search, link text, and selectable code. Downloadable PDFs are retained for printing and annotation and are checked for reading order, document language, bookmarks, metadata, and visual integrity. Accessibility problems can be reported through the repository issue tracker or by email.
